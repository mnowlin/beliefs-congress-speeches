Introduction

Beliefs and Belief Systems

Climate Change in Congress

Data and Analysis

To examine the feasibility of measuring belief systems and information sources among members of Congress, I use floor speeches from the Congressional Record given during three debates over climate and energy legislation. The first is the debate over cap-and-trade in the 111th Congress, the second is the debate over the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) in the 117th Congress, and the third is the debate over the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBB) in the 119th Congress. Below, I describe how the speeches were collected, how they were coded, and the measures used in the analysis.

Congressional Speeches

Congressional speeches were obtained from the daily edition of the Congressional Record using the GovInfo service of the Government Publishing Office, which allows a full-text search of each item printed in the Record. I built two collections of speeches. The first covers 2000 to 2012 and includes any item from the House, the Senate, or the Extensions of Remarks that contains the phrases “climate change,” “global warming,” “greenhouse gas,” “cap and trade,” “national energy tax,” “cap and tax,” or “carbon tax,” or that names one of the major climate bills of the period, such as the American Clean Energy and Security Act (Waxman-Markey). The second covers 2018 to 2026 and uses the same phrases along with the names of the Green New Deal, Build Back Better, the IRA, and the OBBB, as well as phrases that refer to clean energy tax credits in 2025. The Daily Digest, which is the Record’s own summary of each day, was dropped. The searches returned 6,386 items for the first period and 5,388 items for the second.

Each item was then split into speeches. A speech is one member’s turn on the floor, which begins at the line that names the speaker and ends when the next speaker or the presiding officer is recognized. Turns by the presiding officer were dropped, and material that a member asked to have printed in the Record (e.g., bill text, letters, and articles) was removed where it could be identified. Speeches that are published twice in the Record were kept once.

Many of these speeches mention climate change only in passing. As a result, I kept a speech only if it had at least 50 words and met one of three rules. The first rule keeps a speech that has three or more mentions of the climate phrases used in the search and at least two mentions per 1,000 words. The second rule keeps a speech that has two or more mentions when the title of the item in the Record names the topic. The third rule keeps a speech that names one of the climate bills of its period and has three or more mentions of a broader set of energy terms (e.g., clean energy, fossil fuels, and emissions), with at least two mentions per 1,000 words. The third rule is needed because much of the debate over the IRA and the OBBB involved energy tax credits and energy production, and many of those speeches do not use the climate phrases. This resulted in 2,331 speeches from 2000 to 2012 and 2,079 speeches from 2018 to 2026, for a total of 4,410 speeches. The party and state of each speaker come from the GovInfo record for the item, and each speaker was matched to their DW-NOMINATE scores from Voteview.

From these speeches, I coded those given by Democrats and Republicans during the three debates. I define each debate by a window that begins when the legislative vehicle for the bill first moved and ends with the final disposition of the bill. The cap-and-trade window begins with the release of the Waxman-Markey discussion draft on March 31, 2009 and ends on July 22, 2010, when Senate leaders set aside the climate bill. The IRA window begins when the Senate adopted the budget resolution for fiscal year 2022 on August 11, 2021 and ends when the bill was signed on August 16, 2022. The OBBB window begins when the House adopted the budget resolution for fiscal year 2025 on February 25, 2025 and ends when the bill was signed on July 4, 2025. Coding Beliefs and Information Sources

Speeches were coded using a codebook that I developed for this project. Following the Advocacy Coalition Framework, the codebook organizes beliefs into the deep core, policy core, and secondary tiers of a belief system (Jenkins-Smith et al. 2018). The codebook contains 31 concepts, and each concept is written as a proposition with which a speaker can agree or disagree. As shown in Table 2, there are 6 deep core concepts that involve general values such as the role of government and fairness, 12 policy core concepts that involve the climate problem and the costs and benefits of addressing it, and 13 secondary concepts that involve specific policy instruments. The concepts were chosen to reflect how members of Congress argue about climate and energy on the floor, and they were revised over several rounds of pilot coding with a stratified sample of 150 speeches.

The unit of coding is the statement, which is a passage where the speaker expresses a position on one concept (Leifeld 2017). For each statement, the coder records the concept, whether the speaker agrees or disagrees with the proposition, and the intensity of the stance on a four-point scale that ranges from strong disagreement to strong agreement. Beliefs can be understood as probability distributions that have both a central tendency and a degree of uncertainty (Nowlin 2024). To capture uncertainty, the coder also records whether the speaker hedges the stance by qualifying it or presenting it as tentative (e.g., “may,” “might,” or “I think”). Each combination of concept and stance is coded at most once per speech, and each code must be supported by a verbatim quote from the speech of no more than 40 words. Only the positions of the speaker are coded, and a position is not inferred from the party of the speaker or from silence on a concept.

Information sources are coded separately from beliefs. A source is anything outside of the speaker’s own reasoning that is offered as evidence, expertise, or testimony, such as a scientific body, an agency analysis, a news report, or a constituent. For each source, the coder records the type of source using 17 categories (e.g., science, government analysis, business, advocacy groups, the media, and constituents), whether the source is named or generic, the form of the evidence taken from the source, and whether the speaker relies on the source or disputes it. Scientific sources are also coded for whether they are cited for a claim that is in line with the scientific consensus on climate change or a claim that disputes it. Additionally, each source is linked to the concepts that it is used to support, which connects the information that members cite to the beliefs that they express. Finally, for each speech the coder records the bill that is being debated, the stance of the speaker on that bill, and whether climate or energy is the main subject of the speech.

Coding Procedure

The speeches were coded by a large language model (LLM), Claude Opus 5.5 from Anthropic, which follows recent work that uses LLMs as coders that are guided by a codebook (Halterman and Keith 2025). The instructions given to the model were the coding rules of the codebook reproduced word for word, along with the format for the output. The speeches were placed in a random order and divided into batches of 10 speeches. Each batch was coded by a separate instance of the model that had access to only the instructions and the speeches in its batch. As a result, no instance of the model saw the codes produced by another, and each batch contained a mix of debates, parties, and chambers. The text given to the model included the name, party, and state of the speaker, and the instructions direct the coder to not infer a position from party.

After coding, each batch was checked by a script. The script confirmed that every speech was coded once, that all codes used the values allowed by the codebook, and that every quote appears verbatim in the text of the speech. All 9,567 quotes passed this check. Across the 1,281 speeches, the model coded 6,129 statements and 3,438 information sources, and 60 speeches contained no codable statement (e.g., tributes and procedural remarks). The settings that govern randomness in the model could not be fixed, so the codes are not exactly reproducible. For this reason, the raw output of each batch was archived.

Codes produced by an LLM need to be validated against the judgment of human coders. In the pilot, two independent rounds of coding of the same 150 speeches by the model agreed on which concepts were present in a speech, with a median \(\kappa\) of 0.88 across the concepts. However, agreement between two runs of the same model is likely to overstate agreement with human coders. To assess validity, a stratified subset of 54 speeches will be coded independently by two trained human coders using the same codebook, and agreement between the human codes and the model codes will be assessed using Krippendorff’s \(\alpha\) (Krippendorff 2003). Analysis

The analysis is descriptive. To examine whether beliefs can be measured as probability distributions, I examine both the position and the uncertainty of each belief. For position, I use the direction and the intensity of the statements on each concept. For uncertainty, I use the share of statements on each concept that are hedged as well as the dispersion of stances across the speeches that address the concept, since beliefs that are held with less certainty are likely to be expressed less consistently. To examine the information sources cited by members, I use the share of speeches that cite each type of source. I measure sources by their presence in a speech and not by the number of sources cited, since a small number of speeches cite many sources. To compare the three debates, I define the salience of a belief or an information source as the share of speeches in a debate where it appears, and I compare salience across the debates and between Democrats and Republicans. Finally, some speeches address climate or energy only briefly, so I also examine whether the results differ when the analysis is limited to speeches where climate or energy is the main subject.
